A LinkedIn product demo video works best as a native upload, 30 to 90 seconds long, in square (1:1) or 4:5 format, with a hook in the first two seconds, captions for muted autoplay and zooms that keep the interface readable on a phone. Pair it with short post copy that states the problem and one call to action. This guide covers the format, a script you can adapt, and how to produce it from a single screen recording.

What makes LinkedIn different from other channels

LinkedIn viewers are mostly professionals scrolling between meetings. They are not looking for your product. Your demo competes with industry news, job updates and colleagues' posts, and it usually starts playing muted. That leads to four constraints:

  • The hook must be visual and written. Sound will not save the first seconds.
  • The frame must be readable on a phone. Much of the feed is browsed on mobile.
  • The value must be work-related. Time saved, errors avoided, revenue gained.
  • The person matters. Founder-led and employee-led posts often feel more native to LinkedIn than polished brand ads.

A script for a 60-second LinkedIn demo

  1. Hook (0 to 3 seconds): on-screen text and first spoken line name a familiar pain. "Your team spends Friday afternoons copying numbers into a status report."
  2. Turn (3 to 8 seconds): "Here's how we do it in 20 seconds now."
  3. Demo (8 to 50 seconds): the three clicks that produce the result, each zoomed so it is readable.
  4. Payoff (50 to 60 seconds): hold on the finished output. One line on where to try it.

If you appear on webcam, keep the bubble small and in a corner that does not cover the key UI. A face helps on LinkedIn, but the product should fill the frame.

Recording and editing the demo

Record in a clean demo account with realistic data and notifications off. A browser window around 1280 to 1440 pixels wide gives you room to work; the square crop and zooms will handle readability.

Zooms are what make a desktop UI legible in a square feed video. Click & Record, a free Chrome extension, logs each click while you record and turns it into a zoom keyframe afterwards, so the frame pushes in on what you clicked and pans across nearby clicks. You can strengthen zooms for the square export, re-aim them or delete them. The webcam is recorded on its own track, so you can move or resize the bubble to suit a 1:1 frame after recording, or hide it for a product-only cut.

In the editor, a few touches make a LinkedIn demo look polished:

  • Background and padding: place the recording on a brand gradient or image with rounded corners and a shadow.
  • Browser frame: a mock macOS or Windows frame with an editable URL bar, so viewers see your domain rather than an internal link. See adding a background and browser frame.
  • Speed ramps: push any waiting or form filling to 2x to 4x.
  • Trim the start: the first frame should already show the problem.

Captions for muted autoplay

Because feed videos usually start without sound, captions do the talking for most viewers. You have two options:

  • Attach an SRT file when uploading. At the time of writing, LinkedIn supports this for native videos, and viewers can turn captions on or off.
  • Write key messages as on-screen text in the video itself, for the hook and payoff at least.

Click & Record transcribes your narration on your own computer using a built-in Whisper model, with no upload and no per-minute cost. Fix any product names in the cue editor, then export an SRT. The full workflow is in how to add subtitles to a screen recording for free.

Writing the post around the video

The video does not stand alone. The post text above it decides whether people stop to watch.

  • Open with the problem in one short line, matching the video's hook.
  • Add two or three short lines on what changed or what the viewer will see.
  • One call to action: "Link to try it in the comments" or "Reply if you want the template".
  • Tag people only when relevant, such as a teammate who built the feature or a customer who agreed to be mentioned.

Post from a person's profile when you can. A founder or product manager sharing what they built often reads more naturally than a company page announcement.
